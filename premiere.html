Foundations · 1066 – 1707
1066

The Norman Conquest

Britain
William of Normandy defeats King Harold at the Battle of Hastings and takes the English crown. For the next three centuries, the ruling class speaks French while the people speak Old English — and the two languages slowly merge. This is why English has two words for almost everything (ask/demand, freedom/liberty, cow/beef). Modern English is born from this collision.
c. 1590–1616

Shakespeare & the Elizabethan Stage

Britain
In the London of Elizabeth I, theatre becomes a national passion. At the Globe, Shakespeare writes for everyone — from groundlings paying a penny to aristocrats in the galleries — and invents or popularises hundreds of words still used today. Shakespeare is the shared vocabulary of the English-speaking world: quoted, adapted and parodied everywhere from Hollywood to hip-hop.
1620

The Mayflower & the Pilgrims

America
A hundred or so English Puritans cross the Atlantic seeking religious freedom and found Plymouth Colony. Their harsh first winter, and the help of the Wampanoag people, are remembered every year at Thanksgiving. This is America's founding myth — a story of freedom and new beginnings that leaves out the perspective of Native peoples. Understanding it as a myth is understanding America.
1642–1660

Civil War, Cromwell & the Restoration

Britain
Parliament goes to war against King Charles I — and wins. The king is executed in 1649, and for eleven years England is a republic under Oliver Cromwell's stern Puritan rule. Theatres are closed, Christmas is banned. In 1660 the monarchy returns, chastened. Britain executed a king 144 years before France — then invited the monarchy back. This explains the British model: a crown that reigns but does not rule.
1707

The Act of Union

Britain
England (with Wales) and Scotland merge their parliaments to become one kingdom: Great Britain. Ireland joins in 1801, creating the United Kingdom. The key to a universal confusion: England ≠ Britain ≠ the UK. It also explains why Scottish independence is still a live political question today.
Revolutions · 1760 – 1901
c. 1760–1840

The Industrial Revolution

Britain
Steam engines, cotton mills, railways: Britain becomes "the workshop of the world". Millions leave the countryside for smoky industrial cities — Manchester, Birmingham, Glasgow — where fortunes and slums grow side by side. The world you live in — factories, cities, trains, pollution, the working class — starts here. It is also the landscape of Dickens and of Britain's industrial North.
1776

The Declaration of Independence

America
The thirteen American colonies break with Britain, declaring that "all men are created equal" with rights to "Life, Liberty and the pursuit of Happiness". The war that follows creates the United States. These words became the standard America is judged by — invoked by Lincoln, by Martin Luther King, by every movement demanding that the promise finally be kept for everyone.
1861–1865

The Civil War & the Abolition of Slavery

America
Eleven Southern states secede to defend slavery; the North, led by Abraham Lincoln, fights to preserve the Union. Around 750,000 die — more Americans than in both World Wars combined. Slavery is abolished in 1865. The deepest wound in American history. Race, the divide between North and South, debates about Confederate statues — the Civil War is still present in American life.
1845–1852

The Great Irish Famine

Ireland
A disease destroys the potato crop that the Irish poor depend on. Around a million people die of hunger and disease; another million emigrate, most of them to America. Throughout the catastrophe, Ireland — then part of the United Kingdom — continues to export food to Britain. Ireland has never regained its pre-famine population. This is why there are more people of Irish descent in the United States than in Ireland itself — and why Irish nationalism hardened into a demand for independence.
1857–1858

The Indian Rebellion & the Birth of the Raj

India & Empire
Indian soldiers serving a private company — the East India Company, which had ruled much of India for a century — rise in revolt. The rebellion is crushed with great brutality, and the British Crown takes direct control: the Raj begins. Victoria is proclaimed Empress of India in 1876. India was first colonised not by a country but by a corporation — a fact worth pausing on. Indians call 1857 their First War of Independence; British textbooks long called it a "mutiny." Same event, two names.
1837–1901

The Victorian Age

Britain
Under Queen Victoria, Britain rules the largest empire in history — "the empire on which the sun never sets", covering a quarter of the globe. At home: strict morality and respectability on the surface, poverty and child labour underneath. The empire explains why English is a world language and why Britain is multicultural today. The Victorian contrast between appearance and reality feeds its greatest novels.
The Modern World · 1914 – 1969
1914–1918

The Great War

Britain
A generation of young men leaves for the trenches of France and Flanders; nearly a million from Britain and its empire never return. The war poets — Wilfred Owen, Siegfried Sassoon — turn its horror into some of the most powerful poetry in English. Every November, Britain wears the red poppy of remembrance. The Great War shattered Victorian confidence and gave birth to modern literature's darker, fractured voice.
1916–1922

The Easter Rising & the Partition of Ireland

Ireland
At Easter 1916, rebels seize the centre of Dublin and proclaim an Irish Republic. The rising fails within a week, but the execution of its leaders turns them into martyrs — "a terrible beauty is born", writes W.B. Yeats. War and treaty follow: in 1922 the island is divided between an independent Irish Free State and Northern Ireland, which remains in the UK. The first crack in the British Empire, and the origin of a border that still shapes politics today — including the hardest question of Brexit.
1920s

The Roaring Twenties & the Harlem Renaissance

America
Jazz, flappers, Prohibition and gangsters: America roars into modernity. In Harlem, Black writers, musicians and artists — Langston Hughes, Duke Ellington, Zora Neale Hurston — create an explosion of African-American culture. The decade of The Great Gatsby and the birth of the music that would conquer the world. The Harlem Renaissance is the moment Black American voices claim the centre of the stage.
1929

The Wall Street Crash

America
In October 1929 the New York stock market collapses, wiping out fortunes overnight and plunging America — then the world — into the Great Depression. By 1933, one American worker in four is unemployed. The backdrop of Steinbeck's novels and Dorothea Lange's photographs: dust bowls, breadlines, migrant families. It reshaped how Americans think about government, poverty and the American Dream.
1939–1945

World War II, the Blitz & Churchill

Britain
In 1940, Britain stands alone against Nazi Germany. London is bombed for 57 consecutive nights during the Blitz; Churchill's speeches — "we shall fight on the beaches" — hold the nation together until victory in 1945. "The Blitz spirit" — calm courage under pressure — remains Britain's favourite image of itself, invoked in every national crisis (including, ironically, by both sides of the Brexit debate).
1947

Indian Independence & Partition

India & Empire
After decades of nonviolent campaigning led by Gandhi, Britain leaves India. But independence comes with partition: the territory is split into India and Pakistan along religious lines, with the border drawn in a few weeks by a British lawyer who had never been to India. Some fifteen million people are displaced; hundreds of thousands die in the violence. The end of the British Empire begins here, and so does modern South Asian Britain. Gandhi's nonviolence directly inspired Martin Luther King — the thread runs straight to the American civil rights movement.
1948

Windrush & the NHS

Britain
Two births in one year. The ship Empire Windrush brings some 500 Caribbean passengers to London, pioneers of large-scale immigration from the Commonwealth. The same year, the National Health Service opens: free healthcare for all. Modern multicultural Britain and the welfare state start here. The NHS is Britain's "national religion"; the Windrush generation's story runs through contemporary British literature and politics.
1954–1968

The Civil Rights Movement

America
From Brown v. Board of Education (1954), which outlaws school segregation, to Rosa Parks, the Montgomery bus boycott, and Martin Luther King's "I have a dream" (1963) — Black Americans fight to end segregation through nonviolent resistance. King is assassinated in 1968. The essential context for African-American literature and for today's debates on race in America, from Black Lives Matter to the ballot box.
1969

The Moon Landing — and Woodstock

America
In July, Neil Armstrong walks on the Moon: "one giant leap for mankind", watched by 600 million people. A month later, half a million young people gather at Woodstock for three days of music, mud and peace signs. One summer, two Americas: the triumphant superpower of the Space Race, and the rebellious counterculture protesting the Vietnam War. Both are essential to understanding the 1960s.
Our Times · 1979 – Today
1968–1998

The Troubles & the Good Friday Agreement

Ireland
Thirty years of conflict in Northern Ireland between nationalists (mainly Catholic, seeking union with Ireland) and unionists (mainly Protestant, loyal to Britain), with the British Army caught in between. More than 3,500 people die. The Good Friday Agreement of 1998 brings a fragile peace. Peace here is recent — younger than your parents. It rests on an invisible border between North and South, which is exactly what made Brexit so difficult to negotiate.
1979–1990

The Thatcher Years

Britain
Margaret Thatcher, Britain's first female Prime Minister, transforms the country: privatisation, free markets, the crushing of the miners' strike (1984–85). Industrial towns of the North lose their mines and factories — and their way of life. Britain is still arguing about her. The closed pits of the North (think Billy Elliot) and the divide between London and the old industrial regions date from this era.
2001

September 11

America
On 11 September 2001, hijacked planes destroy the World Trade Center in New York and hit the Pentagon, killing nearly 3,000 people. America responds with wars in Afghanistan and Iraq. The moment the 21st century began: security, surveillance, the "war on terror" and a new anxiety in American life and literature all flow from this day.
2008

The Election of Barack Obama

America
Barack Obama becomes the first African-American President of the United States, elected on a message of hope — "Yes we can" — in the middle of a global financial crisis. A landmark in the long story that runs from slavery through the civil rights movement: a moment of promise whose meaning America is still debating.
2016–2020

Brexit

Britain
In June 2016, 52% of British voters choose to leave the European Union. After years of political chaos, the UK formally leaves in 2020 — the first country ever to do so. The defining British question of your lifetime: it divided families, regions and generations, and reopened every old debate about identity — English, Scottish, British, European.
